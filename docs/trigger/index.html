How the Vanuatu cyclone trigger works

Anticipatory action for tropical cyclones — explained

When a dangerous cyclone approaches Vanuatu, help is most valuable before landfall: moving supplies, securing shelters, warning communities. The anticipatory action framework pre-arranges $1.5 million so that money moves automatically when agreed conditions — the trigger — are met, instead of waiting for damage reports afterwards.

The trigger asks one question, in three windows: are at least 15,000 people expected to face hurricane-force winds (64 knots and above)?

⏰ Readiness

3–5 days before

Early heads-up from long-range forecasts. Agencies mobilise staff and pre-position supplies for Shefa, Sanma and Tafea provinces.

Source: RSMC Nadi extended track forecast

⚡ Action

1–3 days before

The main trigger. If forecasts put ≥15,000 people in Shefa, Sanma or Tafea inside the hurricane-force wind area, the full amount is released — with time to act.

Why isn’t rainfall part of the trigger?

Cyclones hurt through wind, rain and storm surge together — so why trigger on wind alone? The working group considered adding a rainfall condition and decided against it, for two reasons. First, simplicity: a trigger everyone can verify at a glance (one number, one map) is easier to operate, audit and trust at 2 a.m. than a multi-condition rule. Second, it didn’t add accuracy: in backtesting, the destructive storms were already caught by wind exposure — adding rainfall thresholds changed little or nothing about which storms activated, while making every activation decision harder to explain. Heavy-rain events without dangerous winds are real, but they are a different hazard, better served by other response tools.

The evidence behind that decision: for every storm in the record we compared each candidate indicator against how many people the storm actually affected, and against whether it led to a CERF humanitarian allocation. Wind exposure — especially at hurricane force — tracks real impact far more closely than rainfall does:

Technical notes: how we reconstructed the historical forecasts

Why JTWC forecasts stand in for RSMC Nadi / VMHS — and how the backtest works

The substitution problem. Operationally, the trigger reads RSMC Nadi and VMHS forecasts. But to test the trigger against history we need the forecasts as they were issued at the time, including the predicted size of the hurricane-force wind area — and neither source archives that. VMGD kindly shared their complete historical archive (9 GB, sixteen seasons): their own Forecast Track Maps record position and intensity category, but the wind boundaries exist only as circles drawn on the map images, not as numbers. The one archived source that does tabulate forecast wind extents is the US Joint Typhoon Warning Center (JTWC), whose official forecasts — with wind radii in four quadrants at 34/50/64 kt — were preserved inside VMGD's own archive for 2012–2024 and in a public research mirror for 2025. JTWC is a close stand-in: it forecasts the same storms at the same times, and its skill is comparable to the operational sources.

From a forecast to a number of people. For every forecast issued (543 forecast cycles across 47 storms), we take only the 24–72 hour portion of the predicted track — the action window; anything closer gives too little warning to act on. We sweep the predicted hurricane-force wind radii along that track segment into a footprint, and count the residents of Shefa, Sanma and Tafea inside it using WorldPop 1 km population data. The observed side is computed the same way from IBTrACS best-track data, so forecast and reality are directly comparable. One conversion matters: JTWC reports 1-minute average winds while Nadi/VMHS use 10-minute averages, so “64 kt” is evaluated at its 1-minute equivalent (≈73 kt, factor 0.88), with the radii interpolated to that contour. We checked the whole pipeline by rebuilding a storm's wind footprint two independent ways (96% area agreement) and by verifying every would-be false alarm against contemporaneous damage reports.

What the archive can't tell us. No forecast records survive for 2006–2011 (six seasons), so the action trigger simply cannot be scored there — two storms in those years (Gene 2008, Atu 2011) brought hurricane-force winds to or beside the trigger provinces and caused documented damage, so the statistics above carry one assumed activation for that period. Season 2005 is excluded outright: forecast files exist for that single year alone among 2003–2011, with unclear provenance. And the readiness window (3–5 days) is not assessed at all — archived long-range forecasts with wind sizes are too sparse before 2017 to score it fairly. These gaps are why the overall return period is reported as “≤” rather than an exact value.
