The trigger

Each of the three triggers asks the same question at a different time: are at least 15,000 people expected to be exposed, or have they been exposed, to maximum sustained winds of 64 knots (about 119 km/h) or more?

TriggerLead timeWind speed Number of people exposedProvincesData source
Readiness 72 to 120 hours ≥ 64 knots ≥ 15,000 people Shefa, Sanma, Tafea RSMC Nadi extended track forecast
Action 24 to 72 hours VMHS track forecast
Observational n/a All of Vanuatu VMHS observed track

Lead time is the time before landfall. Wind speeds are 10-minute sustained winds, as used by RSMC Nadi and VMHS. No anticipatory activities begin in the 24 hours before landfall (NDMO's operational cut-off); activities under the observational trigger start after landfall and NDMO's all-clear. Triggers are monitored from 1 October to 31 May. CERF has pre-arranged up to $1.5 million, disbursed once, on the first trigger reached: a readiness activation authorises the readiness share of the funds, an action or observational activation all of them.

How well it performs

The framework set the trigger to catch most of the cyclones for which international humanitarian assistance was needed, with CERF's past allocations for cyclones in Vanuatu as the measure, while activating about once in four years.

Why wind, and not rainfall

Cyclones do harm through wind, rain and storm surge together. The working group considered adding a rainfall condition and decided against it. A trigger on one number is easier to operate, check and explain; and in the backtest the destructive storms were already caught by wind exposure, so a rainfall threshold changed little or nothing about which storms activated it.

For every storm in the 2006–2025 record, each candidate indicator against the people the storm affected and against whether it led to a CERF allocation: exposure to hurricane-force winds tracks both far more closely than rainfall does.

Technical notes: how the historical forecasts were reconstructed

Why JTWC forecasts stand in for RSMC Nadi and VMHS, and how the backtest works

The substitution problem. Operationally, the trigger reads RSMC Nadi and VMHS forecasts. But to test the trigger against history we need the forecasts as they were issued at the time, including the predicted size of the hurricane-force wind area, and neither source archives that. VMGD kindly shared their complete historical archive (9 GB, sixteen seasons): their own Forecast Track Maps record position and intensity category, but the wind boundaries exist only as circles drawn on the map images, not as numbers. The one archived source that does tabulate forecast wind extents is the US Joint Typhoon Warning Center (JTWC), whose official forecasts (with wind radii in four quadrants at 34/50/64 kt) were preserved inside VMGD's own archive for 2012–2024 and in a public research mirror for 2025. JTWC is a close stand-in: it forecasts the same storms at the same times, and its skill is comparable to the operational sources.

From a forecast to a number of people. For every forecast issued (543 forecast cycles across 47 storms), we take only the 24–72 hour portion of the predicted track: the action window. We sweep the predicted hurricane-force wind radii along that track segment into a footprint, and count the residents of Shefa, Sanma and Tafea inside it using WorldPop 1 km population data. The observed side is computed the same way from IBTrACS best-track data, so forecast and reality are directly comparable. One conversion matters: JTWC reports 1-minute average winds while Nadi and VMHS use 10-minute averages, so “64 kt” is evaluated at its 1-minute equivalent (≈73 kt, factor 0.88), with the radii interpolated to that contour. We checked the whole pipeline by rebuilding a storm's wind footprint two independent ways (96% area agreement) and by checking every would-be false alarm against damage reports from the time.

What the archive can't tell us. No forecast records survive for 2006–2011 (six seasons), so the action trigger cannot be scored there. Two storms in those years (Gene 2008, Atu 2011) brought hurricane-force winds to or beside the trigger provinces and caused documented damage, so the statistics carry one assumed activation for that period. Season 2005 is excluded: forecast files exist for that single year alone among 2003–2011, with unclear provenance. The readiness window (3–5 days) is not assessed at all: archived long-range forecasts with wind sizes are too sparse before 2017 to score it fairly. These gaps are why the overall return period is reported as “≤” rather than an exact value.
