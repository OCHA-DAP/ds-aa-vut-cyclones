How the Vanuatu cyclone trigger works

Anticipatory action for tropical cyclones — explained

When a dangerous cyclone approaches Vanuatu, help is most valuable before landfall: moving supplies, securing shelters, warning communities. The anticipatory action framework pre-arranges $1.5 million so that money moves automatically when agreed conditions — the trigger — are met, instead of waiting for damage reports afterwards.

The trigger asks one question, in three windows: are at least 15,000 people expected to face hurricane-force winds (64 knots and above)?

⏰ Readiness

3–5 days before

Early heads-up from long-range forecasts. Agencies mobilise staff and pre-position supplies for Shefa, Sanma and Tafea provinces.

Source: RSMC Nadi extended track forecast

⚡ Action

1–3 days before

The main trigger. If forecasts put ≥15,000 people in Shefa, Sanma or Tafea inside the hurricane-force wind area, the full amount is released — with time to act.

Source: VMHS track forecast

🔭 Observational

as it happens

The safety net, and it covers all of Vanuatu: if a storm actually exposes ≥15,000 people anywhere in the country, the framework activates even if the forecasts missed it.

Source: VMHS observed track

Why isn’t rainfall part of the trigger?

Cyclones hurt through wind, rain and storm surge together — so why trigger on wind alone? The working group considered adding a rainfall condition and decided against it, for two reasons. First, simplicity: a trigger everyone can verify at a glance (one number, one map) is easier to operate, audit and trust at 2 a.m. than a multi-condition rule. Second, it didn’t add accuracy: in backtesting, the destructive storms were already caught by wind exposure — adding rainfall thresholds changed little or nothing about which storms activated, while making every activation decision harder to explain. Heavy-rain events without dangerous winds are real, but they are a different hazard, better served by other response tools.
